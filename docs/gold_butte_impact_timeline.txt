GOLD BUTTE: A COLD-EARTH RECASTING OF THE MARS-EARTH GRAZING IMPACT
====================================================================

The cleanest way to picture the event is as a transition through three regimes:

1. For most of the preceding day, Gold Butte remains recognizable terrain
   undergoing an extraordinary but slow planetary tide.
2. During the final two to four hours, “geology” begins to fail: the lithosphere
   fractures and moves on scales of hundreds of metres to kilometres.
3. Roughly 20–90 minutes after first contact on the far side, the antipodal shock
   converts the site from a landscape into a Lagrangian parcel of fragmented,
   shocked material. From then onward the SPH bulk motion is more meaningful than
   any reconstruction of the original summit.

I place the collision near 50 degrees S in the southern Indian Ocean so that Gold
Butte is close to the antipode. This is a geographical rotation of the simulated
encounter, whose original contact geometry was nearly equatorial. It preserves the
gross energetics and antipodal timing, but not an exact pre-impact sky track.

The Gold Butte summit has dark, blocky rubble and low rockwork in the foreground,
grassy shoulders falling toward small ponds, and an unusually open prairie
horizon. The Sweet Grass Hills rise almost 3,000 feet above the plains and are
resistant Eocene igneous intrusions surrounded by older sedimentary rock. That
resistant summit would probably outlast the surrounding slopes during the early
deformation—but only by minutes or hours.

https://sonnybou.ca/ssbou2016/goldbutte.html
https://www.blm.gov/visit/sweet-grass-hills-1

THE GOLD BUTTE TIMELINE
-----------------------

t = -24 to -12 hours: an ominously intact world

Gold Butte still looks like Gold Butte. Wind crosses the grass; the ponds and
distant prairie remain visible; the sky is not yet on fire.

Mars would probably remain below the southern horizon in the intended antipodal
mapping. At contact it is almost directly beneath the observer, and a celestial
direction near 50 degrees south never rises for an observer near 49 degrees north.
Around twelve hours before impact it might pass within several degrees of the
southern horizon, but its roughly two-degree disk would still be hidden. Thus
there need be no enormous red planet visibly descending through the sky over
Montana.

The first unmistakable effects are geodetic:

- At -24 hours the solid Earth’s equilibrium tidal displacement is of order metres.
- By -12 hours it is tens of metres at the tidal extrema.
- Earth’s rotation carries Gold Butte through different parts of the tidal figure.
  In one illustrative orientation, the summit goes from the outward far-side bulge
  at -24 hours through a shallow inward phase near -12 hours, before approaching
  the rapidly growing antipodal bulge during the final several hours.

Because the deformation has a planetary wavelength, Gold Butte and the surrounding
prairie initially move almost together. A person does not feel a five-metre rise
spread over hours. Survey instruments, gravimeters, well levels and long-baseline
GPS would register impossible changes. Lakes and reservoirs begin unusual
seiching. Ocean basins elsewhere experience tides many metres high and badly out
of phase with the forcing.

Faults already near failure would be triggered. There would be scattered
earthquakes, landslides and changes in springs, but the North American craton would
not yet be liquefied or incandescent. Tidal deformation itself does not heat the
summit to thousands of kelvin.


t = -12 to -6 hours: planetary tide becomes a geological event

The maximum equilibrium solid tide grows from about 20 to 100 metres. Its changing
direction bends the lithosphere on a timescale of hours.

On Gold Butte, the strongest igneous blocks remain intact longer than the
sedimentary foothills, but talus slopes begin moving. Existing joints open and
close. Repeated regional earthquakes knock down structures and raise dust over the
plains. Ponds spill and oscillate. The horizon subtly changes elevation as the
whole region tilts by tens of microradians.

The atmosphere is disturbed primarily from below: moving terrain, large-scale
pressure tides, ocean-atmosphere coupling, dust and volcanic or fault-related gas
release. At the precise antipodal axis the horizontal tidal force is relatively
small, so the local wind need not yet be the worst on Earth. The scene could retain
an unsettling normality between earthquakes.


t = -6 to -4 hours: the last recognizably terrestrial interval

The global equilibrium displacement scale passes 100 metres and reaches roughly
250 metres. The actual response lags because the forcing is accelerating, but it
is now comparable to Earth’s elastic normal-mode timescale. The gravest spheroidal
mode of ordinary Earth has a period of about 54 minutes; the approaching Mars is
beginning to force the planet on similarly short timescales.

Normal-mode reference:
https://doi.org/10.1002/2017RG000566

This is no longer a sequence of ordinary earthquakes. Large regions are being
loaded coherently. Fault-system ruptures are secondary details riding on a global
degree-two deformation.

From the summit, the visible effects would be:

- Continuous rockfalls rather than isolated ones.
- Broad prairie surfaces rolling and tilting.
- Dust curtains along the foothills and distant fault scarps.
- Water behaving as if the basin itself were being tipped.
- A persistent low roar from moving rock, wind and seismic activity.

The Sun or stars may still be visible. There is still no direct impact flash
because the future contact region is on the other side of the planet.


t = -4 to -2 hours: Gold Butte begins to lose its independence

In the illustrative antipodal geometry, Gold Butte is now entering the rapidly
growing outward far-side bulge. An instantaneous elastic model suggests a local
displacement rising through roughly a hundred metres toward a kilometre. The real
response will be smaller and phase-lagged, but vertical velocities of centimetres
to perhaps tenths of a metre per second become plausible even before seismic
shaking is included.

The distinction between the igneous butte and its sedimentary surroundings still
matters locally—the butte can fragment into larger, stronger blocks—but it no
longer controls the motion. The entire lithospheric column is being bent.

Human survival is essentially over through ordinary mechanisms: violent
earthquakes, slope collapse, exposed utilities and pipelines, dust, fires and
atmospheric disruption. Nevertheless, the rock itself can still be cold and solid
in many places. This is an important correction to the original hot SPH initial
condition.


t = -2 hours to -30 minutes: geology gives way to continuum mechanics

The formal equilibrium tide rises from about one kilometre to an absurd value
larger than the lithosphere can support. That is not a prediction of a
14-kilometre-high Gold Butte. It signals that linear tide theory has failed.

The lithosphere ruptures on continental scales. The mantle beneath responds as a
rapidly deforming viscoelastic or fluid body. Gold Butte may persist briefly as a
raft or collection of fractured blocks, but it is no longer stationary relative
to a stable crust.

Even now the direct differential gravitational acceleration is not “weightlessness”:
at -1 hour it is only a few thousandths of g. The destructive effect is the
accumulated strain, changing curvature and dynamically excited planetary motion,
not Mars simply lifting people off the ground.

The ground’s slow rise begins accelerating and reversing as the tidal bulge can no
longer keep pace with Mars. The atmosphere is dragged by terrain moving beneath
it. Winds and pressure fluctuations become extreme, but there is still no
instantaneous thermal pulse from the collision site.


t = 0: first contact, unseen

Mars begins grazing Earth in the southern Indian Ocean at roughly 9–10 km/s.

At Gold Butte there is no simultaneous flash, sound or seismic jolt. Light cannot
pass through Earth, and mechanical information has not arrived. The observer
continues experiencing the already enormous tidal deformation.

The collision’s relative kinetic-energy scale is approximately

    E_collision ~ 2.7 x 10^31 J,

roughly one-eighth of Earth’s gravitational binding energy. How much becomes
shock heat, global oscillation, ejecta or Mars-remnant orbital energy is the
central hydrodynamic question.


t = +15 to +30 minutes: the first signal through the planet

The fastest compressional phases traverse Earth’s core in approximately twenty
minutes. Ordinary terrestrial P waves range from roughly 6 km/s in crust to about
13 km/s in the core; a PREM travel-time calculation gives the same order.

Seismic reference:
https://www.usgs.gov/programs/earthquake-hazards/seismographs-keeping-track-earthquakes

This first arrival would not resemble the onset of a conventional earthquake. It
is the leading edge of a finite, planetary-scale source. The ground abruptly
changes from slow tidal motion to violent compression and rebound.

Antipodal focusing tends to concentrate seismic energy, although real lateral
structure smears the ideal focus. Even for Chicxulub-scale sources, numerical
calculations find substantial focusing; applying their exact amplification factors
to a Mars-sized collision would be unjustified because the response here is
nonlinear.

Antipodal-focusing reference:
https://academic.oup.com/gji/article/187/1/529/564818

The summit’s jointed rock shatters. Blocks are thrown, compressed and rotated.
“Standing on Gold Butte” ceases to have a physical meaning, although we can
continue following the original material.


t = +30 to +75 minutes: the landscape is erased

This is where the SPH calculation becomes most useful for bulk motion.

Relative to first contact, the simulation finds:

- About +35 minutes: global surface radial RMS motion peaks near 440 m/s.
- About +40 minutes: the antipodal surrogate first enters a strong,
  multi-gigapascal pressure excursion.
- About +70 minutes: global three-dimensional residual surface speed peaks near
  890 m/s.
- About +70 minutes: the antipodal parcel reaches pressure changes of order
  10 GPa in the coarse model.

These are not earthquake ground velocities. A normal seismic instrument and
moment-magnitude description are no longer meaningful. Material moving vertically
at 400–900 m/s has a ballistic height scale of tens of kilometres if temporarily
unsupported.

The cold cratonic rock does not necessarily melt at the first shock front. A
10–30 GPa pulse will pulverize it and heat it strongly, but complete melting of
coherent granitoid depends on shock path, porosity and repeated compression. The
robust conclusion is fragmentation and loss of stratigraphic context, not an
exact local temperature.

Surface waves travelling at a few kilometres per second require roughly one to
two hours to converge from the impact side around the circumference. Here they
overlap with global normal modes and hydrodynamic deformation.

Surface-wave reference:
https://earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/events/1906calif/18april/earthwaves.php


t = +1 to +3 hours: the observer becomes an astrophysical tracer

The original Gold Butte material is now part of a fragmented, accelerating mixture.
In the SPH surrogate it:

- Moves at roughly 1–1.6 km/s relative to smooth rotation.
- Experiences a peak pressure of order several tens of GPa around +1.5 to +2 hours.
- Is transported hundreds of kilometres radially relative to its pre-impact
  position and temporarily overtaken by other outer material.

That radial excursion should not be interpreted literally as “Gold Butte drops
600 kilometres through an intact mantle.” It means that the parcel has been
engulfed by the rearranging outer planet; its percentile within the remnant changes
dramatically.

The sky now catches up with the ground. Fast ejecta and impact-generated vapor can
travel globally on suborbital timescales of approximately one hour. Re-entering
rock, expanding steam and silicate vapor turn the atmosphere luminous and
increasingly opaque. There may therefore be a striking interval in which the
antipodal seismic catastrophe arrives before the main visible skyfire.

Only a few ten-thousandths of the collision energy is comparable to the few x
10^27 J required to heat and vaporize the modern ocean. An intact ocean at this
stage is implausible, although this simulation cannot determine exactly how quickly
each basin vaporizes. Very-large-impact calculations independently produce global
ejecta blankets and rock-vapor atmospheres, even for impactors smaller than Mars.

Very-large-impact reference:
https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0032063305001194


t = +3 to +8 hours: a ringing planetary remnant

Mars has passed and is receding, so the externally imposed tide weakens. Earth’s
own oscillation now dominates.

At roughly four hours after contact, outer-material residual speeds in the SPH run
remain around 500 m/s. At approximately seven hours they are still around 250 m/s.
The planet is alternately oblate, elongated and irregular, with shocks and pressure
waves repeatedly traversing it. The familiar 54-minute normal mode is only a guide:
melting, rotation, mass exchange and large deformation shift the actual spectrum.

The original summit material may move outward again and return near the remnant’s
nominal surface, but it is no longer a coherent hill. It may be dispersed among
melt, fragmented crust and fallback ejecta.

The atmosphere is a mixture of surviving air, steam, impact gases and silicate
vapor. Some is escaping; much remains gravitationally bound. Day and night are
largely replaced by an incandescent, opaque canopy.


t = +8 to +24 hours: damping, fallback and loss of geography

The Earth remnant remains bound and massive, but “surface” denotes a time-dependent
photosphere or outer material envelope, not land and sea.

By about +17 hours the tracked antipodal parcel’s residual motion has declined to
roughly 90 m/s in the model. At +24 hours, tens to perhaps 100 m/s is a defensible
scale, depending on which outer material is sampled. This is strong damping
relative to the first few hours but still astrophysically violent.

Mars survives the first passage as a major remnant and is receding toward a distant
orbital turning point. A Mars-derived secondary is forming nearby. Their future
close encounters matter, but they do not restore a geological surface during this
first day.

Giant-impact calculations commonly find that a standard Mars-sized impact leaves
most of Earth’s mantle molten, although the degree of mixing can remain incomplete.
That is the appropriate +24-hour picture here: a hot, strongly oscillating Earth
remnant with a vapor-rich envelope and widespread fallback, not a damaged but
recognizable North America.

Mantle-melting reference:
https://authors.library.caltech.edu/records/agttc-91h97


THE MONTHS-LONG OPTICAL APPROACH OF MARS
----------------------------------------

The eeriest feature of the approach is not initially rapid motion. It is that
Mars remains nearly fixed among the stars while steadily swelling.

The SPH calculation itself begins only two hours before contact and contains no
Sun. It therefore does not specify a unique heliocentric orbit months before the
collision. The reconstruction below is a kinematic backward extension of the
simulation's incoming Earth-Mars state, whose hyperbolic excess speed is about
1.91 km/s, embedded in the same August 17 sky geometry used elsewhere in this
note. The distances and angular sizes are useful consequences of that extension.
The month-scale constellation, elongation and phase are one physically plausible
solar-system realization, not a unique result of the SPH initial conditions.
Beyond Earth's Hill sphere a proper reconstruction would require a solar-system
N-body trajectory.

The assumed incoming asymptote places Mars close to the celestial equator in or
near Virgo. Its changing illumination is estimated with a cold, present-like Mars
and a simple Lambertian scattering law. The listed magnitudes are consequently
order-of-magnitude visual estimates rather than predictions of weather, dust or
surface photometry.

Six to five months before impact: an impossible opposition

In February, Mars is an extraordinary orange "star." It rises during the evening
and remains visible through most of the night. To most unaided eyes it is still
point-like, although unusually steady and intensely coloured. In binoculars or
any telescope it is already unmistakably a globe: about Jupiter's apparent
diameter and nearly twice the largest Mars normally appears from Earth.

Near March 20 it reaches opposition. It rises near sunset, culminates due south
around midnight at approximately 41 degrees altitude as seen from Gold Butte, and
sets near sunrise. It is nearly fully illuminated and somewhat brighter than
Venus ever becomes. In a dark prairie sky it can cast a subtle shadow.

This opposition is profoundly abnormal to astronomers. Instead of executing an
ordinary Martian retrograde loop, it moves only a few arcseconds per day while its
range collapses rapidly. Radar, parallax and spectroscopy establish the collision
course long before the public can resolve its disk. By this point there is no
scientifically credible interpretation in which this is merely an unusually
favourable apparition of normal Mars.

Four to three months before impact: a world rather than a star

During April and May the Sun begins catching up with Mars from the west. Mars
becomes an evening object, still culminating in the southern sky but setting
progressively earlier. Its phase diminishes from almost full to gibbous.

By April its diameter exceeds one arcminute. Exceptionally sharp unaided eyes may
notice that it is not a point. By May, at about 1.5 arcminutes, it looks like a
minute ochre bead and scarcely scintillates. The polar cap, Syrtis Major and other
large albedo markings are far easier to see telescopically than in any historical
apparition.

Mars's 24 hour 37 minute rotation has a peculiar visual consequence. At roughly
the same local hour on successive nights almost the same hemisphere returns,
displaced by only a few degrees, but the entire globe is perceptibly larger over
longer intervals. The resemblance from night to night emphasizes the growth.

Two months before impact: a tiny half-Moon

By mid-June Mars is approximately half illuminated and 2.2 arcminutes wide. It
culminates near the end of the afternoon or around sunset and sets near midnight.
To the unaided eye it is now a resolved, asymmetric world: a tiny orange half-Moon
about one-thirteenth the Moon's diameter. Its integrated light is several times
brighter than Venus, although that light is spread over a visible surface.

Its location still changes remarkably little. At two months the trajectory
produces only about 15 arcseconds of geocentric motion per day, much less than the
Moon's roughly 13 degrees per day. The Sun's seasonal progress changes when Mars
rises and sets more conspicuously than Mars changes position among the stars.

One month before impact: the daylight crescent

During July Mars changes from a half-lit world into a broad crescent. At the
one-month mark it is 4.3 arcminutes across, about one-seventh the Moon's width. It
is easy to find in daylight once its position is known. After sunset it hangs in
the south or southwest as a copper-coloured miniature planet, with a brilliant
sunward limb and a darker reddish remainder.

Earthshine increasingly illuminates the Martian night hemisphere. This does not
make the dark portion as bright as the sunlit crescent, but it can complete the
impression of a ghostly round globe, especially through optical aid. Mars's motion
against the stars is still only about one arcminute per day. It returns night
after night almost in the same place while continuing to enlarge.

The final two weeks: naked-eye planetary geography

On August 3 Mars is about 8.8 arcminutes wide, nearly one-third the Moon's
diameter. The crescent shape and its two horns are obvious without optical aid.
Broad differences in surface tone may be suspected across the dim globe, although
glare, atmospheric seeing and any Martian dust make exact naked-eye detail
uncertain.

On August 10 it is more than half the Moon's diameter and shines near magnitude
-8.3. Its geocentric motion has risen to about 0.2 degree per day, finally obvious
from one evening to the next. Earth is now inside the region where its tide on
Mars exceeds the solar tide. That does not instantly disrupt Mars, but increasing
quakes, slope failure and dust during the last days are plausible. The present SPH
initial conditions do not model this months-long tidal preparation of Mars.

On August 14 Mars equals or slightly exceeds the Moon's diameter, although only
about one-tenth of its face is directly illuminated. The dim earthlit hemisphere
completes the globe. The formerly almost stationary planet now moves nearly a
degree per day relative to the stars.

The final visible passage

At local noon on August 15, t=-48 hours, Mars is already 0.765 degree wide, about
1.5 lunar diameters. It is a resolved daylight crescent in the southeast at an
altitude of about 32 degrees. It culminates that afternoon near 41 degrees
altitude and sets in the west shortly after local-solar 20:20.

It returns on August 16 substantially enlarged. At its final culmination it is
about 1.47 degrees wide. At the final western setting, ending near local-solar
20:34, its disk is about 1.87 degrees wide: 3.6 lunar diameters. The setting lasts
nearly twelve minutes from the lower limb touching the horizon until the upper
limb disappears.

Earth's gravitational deflection is now visible directly. The formerly stationary
world moves roughly 0.4 degree per hour, comparable to the Moon's ordinary motion.
Its phase fraction also bottoms out and begins increasing slightly as the path
curves, turning the final thin crescent into a slowly re-waxing one.

Mars then remains below Gold Butte's horizon for the last 15.5 hours. Its visible
disk is gone while the tides, earthquakes and deformation of Earth continue to
accelerate. Maximum impact occurs around local noon on August 17 with Mars hidden
beneath the northern horizon.


DETAILED PRE-IMPACT EXPANSION: t = -48 HOURS TO -1 HOUR
-------------------------------------------------------

Clock convention and native geometry

For this expansion, t=0 is the moment of maximum impact coupling and closest
Earth-source/Mars-source COM approach at simulation time 2.1667 hours. First
material contact occurs about 15 minutes earlier, at t=-0.25 hour. The Earth spin
period is the simulated 23.9344696 hours.

The following visibility trace uses the repository's native encounter orientation,
not the deliberately rotated exact-antipode thought experiment. At maximum impact,
the Earth-Mars line points toward approximately 73.2 degrees E and essentially the
equator in the simulation's geographic labels. Gold Butte is about 131 degrees from
that sub-Mars point: nearly opposite in longitude, but roughly 49 degrees north of
the exact antipode. That difference is why Mars is visible during the two preceding
days and then sets, rather than remaining beneath Gold Butte throughout the approach.

Local noon is interpreted as apparent solar noon. On August 15-17, sunrise and
sunset at Gold Butte are approximately 04:56 and 19:04 local solar time. Mountain
Daylight Time is roughly 1 hour 25-30 minutes later. Thus the final 20:34 solar-time
setting is near 22:00 MDT. If "local noon" instead means 12:00 MDT, the elapsed
simulation intervals are unchanged but the illumination times shift by about 1.5
hours.

Mars's last direct disappearance is therefore 15 hours 26 minutes before maximum
impact. The final geometric setting takes 11.5 minutes from lower-limb contact to
upper-limb disappearance. Its topocentric distance is approximately 201,000 km and
its diameter is 1.866 degrees: 3.6 lunar diameters and nearly thirteen lunar-disk
areas. Refraction and the actual western horizon could shift final visibility by
about 5-10 minutes, changing the diameter only to roughly 1.86-1.88 degrees.

Mars remains a waxing crescent. The Sun-Mars elongation grows only from about 38 to
41 degrees; the illuminated fraction changes from about 10.6 percent at t=-48 hours
to 12.2 percent at final setting. In a cold, present-like optical recasting, it is a
large dusty copper crescent surrounding an earthlit, dim red night hemisphere. A
rough Lambertian estimate gives integrated magnitude near -10 at t=-48 hours and
-12.2 before horizon extinction at final setting. These optical estimates explicitly
discard the SPH profile's artificial 2000 K starting surface.

The phase barely changes; the size does. At the two culminations, separated by about
24.16 hours, Mars grows from 0.799 to 1.467 degrees, an 84 percent increase. Between
successive settings it grows from 0.887 to 1.866 degrees, more than doubling. Its
motion relative to the stars is initially only about 0.03 degree per hour, rises to
0.09 degree per hour at the first setting, and reaches about 0.40 degree per hour at
the final setting. The Moon normally moves about 0.55 degree per hour relative to
the stars. Mars therefore rises, culminates, and sets much like a star for most of
the interval. It appears to remain nearly fixed in the stellar sphere while swelling.

The first and second culminations occur at 14:21 and 14:31, only ten minutes apart
in local solar time. Mars moves roughly 3.5 degrees east against the stars between
them, much less than the Moon's approximately 13 degrees per day. Its simulated
24.623-hour spin also presents nearly the same hemisphere at successive
culminations, shifted by only about seven degrees. The result is the uncanny return
of almost the same face, nearly twice as wide.

Typical crustal-earthquake stress drops are about 0.1-10 MPa. Before t~-12 hours,
Mars primarily triggers faults already storing tectonic stress; it advances their
failure times but does not supply the earthquake energy. Around t=-6 to -4 hours,
the imposed tide itself enters earthquake stress-drop territory. From then onward,
separate earthquakes become superposed on forced regional and planetary deformation.

The local fault inventory

The immediate Sweet Grass Hills are ordinarily quiet. An analysis of the Montana
Bureau of Mines and Geology 1982-2015 catalog finds no located earthquakes within
25 km of Gold Butte, six within 50 km (mostly magnitude 2-3), and a magnitude 4.4
event about 58 km away in 1994. Detection thresholds varied, so this does not exclude
microseismicity, but it confirms that Gold Butte is not normally an active seismic
center.

The first likely local structures are two short, subparallel faults north and
northwest of Gold Butte. They strike about 58 degrees (northeast-southwest), appear
to be normal faults down to the northwest, and have a combined end-to-end length of
about 5.7 km. One has a nearly continuous "fresh" scarp, the other a "prominent"
scarp; the southern fault may preserve older left-lateral motion. Their dip,
recurrence, and last-event age are unknown. Whole-length rupture corresponds only
approximately to an Mw~5-5.7 event and is not guaranteed.

Middle Butte also contains cooling joints, radial and concentric dikes and sills,
intrusive-country-rock contacts, mineralized zones, old mine workings, and abundant
angular talus. The broader hills contain northeast- and northwest-striking faults
probably inherited from Precambrian basement fabrics and repeatedly reactivated.
Longer regional structures include fault-bounded blocks of the Sweetgrass Arch and
the Pendroy, Scapegoat-Bannatyne, Cat Creek, and Havre structural trends. They are
not all active surface faults at Gold Butte, but they can transmit or host larger
regional events as the forcing grows.

Earthquake progression

t=-48 to -36 hours: The tide is only 4-8 lunar tides. Increased microearthquakes on
fluid-filled fractures are plausible, with occasional M1-M2 events and perhaps an
M3 on a fault already at failure. A shallow M2 could sound like a distant thump; an
M3 might dislodge unstable talus. The summit remains mechanically normal.

t=-36 to -30 hours: At 8-13 kPa, a regional microearthquake swarm becomes likely.
Most events remain below human perception. There may be isolated audible cracking,
small rockfalls, and an occasional M3-M4 regionally. This is triggering, not crustal
failure.

t=-30 to -24 hours: At 13-30 kPa, locally felt M2.5-M4 events become plausible.
Unstable mine workings and talus fail first; saturated pond margins and loose
glacial deposits slump. Springs and wells change as fractures open and close. A
nearby M4 produces several seconds of sharp shaking but does not erase the hill.

t=-24 to -18 hours: The response becomes a multi-fault episode: frequent
microearthquakes, repeated M3-M4 events, possible M4-M5 ruptures within tens of
kilometres, and long-period arrivals from larger events farther west. Slip begins
patchily on the mapped Gold Butte faults; some may be aseismic. A complete local
rupture would be roughly M5-class and would cause violent summit rockfall, but is
not deterministic.

t=-18 to -15.4 hours: While the final enormous Mars crescent is still visible,
microseismic activity is nearly continuous. Felt M3-M4 events may recur on
tens-of-minutes scales, an M5 local event becomes credible, and regional M5-M6
events can send long-period motion through the hills. Sharp local jolts, distant
rolling waves, slow tidal tilt, and persistent rockfall become superposed.

t=-15.4 to -12 hours: After Mars has set, the forcing doubles toward 0.1 MPa.
Repeated M3-M5 events, patchy failure of both local scarps, contact-zone cracking,
and rock avalanches are plausible. The visible cause has disappeared, but the
seismic sequence accelerates.

t=-12 to -8 hours: Multiple local and regional structures can be active at once.
M4-M6 events across northern Montana or southern Alberta become plausible, while
large earthquakes at critically stressed global plate boundaries send additional
waves through the site. The record comprises seconds-long local shocks,
minutes-long regional and teleseismic surface waves, and hours-long forced tilt.

t=-8 to -6 hours: With 0.3-0.5 MPa forcing, local M4-M5 events and regional M6-class
events are credible, but magnitude depends on available rupture area rather than
tidal stress alone. Many faults slip aseismically. Talus slopes become continuously
mobile and already loosened sedimentary foothills fail more readily than diorite.

t=-6 to -4 hours: The load approaches 1-2 MPa. The two mapped faults almost
certainly move if mechanically continuous; old basement structures, dikes, and
intrusive contacts reactivate; new fractures bridge between them. Individual
earthquakes still occur, but they ride on continuous forced motion.

t=-4 to -2 hours: Several-MPa forcing produces distributed rupture, repeated slip,
new fault growth, large-scale lithospheric bending, and increasing lower-crustal
participation. M6-M7 regional ruptures are possible on sufficiently long inherited
structures, but the dominant local phenomenon is simultaneous motion on many
orientations, not one giant Gold Butte earthquake.

t=-2 to -1 hour: The formal stress reaches ordinary to high earthquake stress-drop
values and linear elasticity is failing. Fault friction is no longer the sole
control; intact bridges break, deep crust deforms, and the ground acquires a
coherent forced velocity. Mainshock, aftershock, and tidal deformation cease to be
cleanly separable categories.

Weather and atmospheric response

The atmosphere partly follows the changing gravitational equipotential, so the raw
solid-tide height is not an atmospheric pressure change. A simple hydrostatic
residual estimate gives:

 Time       Pressure-tide scale       Interpretation
 ---------------------------------------------------------------
 -48 h          <0.1 hPa              hidden in weather noise
 -24 h          ~0.4 hPa              globally detectable
 -15.4 h        ~1 hPa                measurable, still weather-like
 -12 h          ~2 hPa                suspicious coherent changes
  -8 h          ~5 hPa                weather-system scale
  -6 h         ~10 hPa                major change in a few hours
  -4 h         ~25 hPa                severe nonmeteorological forcing
  -3 h         tens of hPa            hydrostatic estimate degrading
  -2 h onward   nonlinear             dynamically coupled atmosphere

These are order-of-magnitude scales; normal modes, topography, and ocean-atmosphere
coupling can change amplitude and phase. For the first day, August weather remains
weather: a representative clear summit afternoon is in the upper 60s or low 70s F,
with an exposed breeze and a night in the 40s. The Mars tide does not directly heat
the air and produces no pre-impact aurora or global firelight.

During the final visible day, a 0.4-1 hPa tidal pressure signal is locally
indistinguishable from ordinary weather, though meteorologists recognize its global
coherence. After Mars sets, the signal grows through 1-5 hPa: winds change without
a front, dust rises from several valleys at once, and orographic clouds form and
dissipate at the wrong times. By t=-6 hours, a roughly 10 hPa change over a few
hours resembles a rapidly deepening storm without normal synoptic structure. By
t=-4 hours, 20-30 hPa mechanical forcing, seismic dust, fires, landslides, and moving
terrain make ordinary forecasting meaningless. Temperature changes remain modest
until impact; wind, pressure, dust, and ground motion are the hazards.

When the anomaly becomes undeniable

Astronomically, it is undeniable before this interval: at t=-48 hours Mars is
already 0.765 degree wide, a resolved daylight crescent larger than the Moon.
Globally, extraordinary ocean tides, synchronized seismicity, and geodetic motion
make the crisis obvious by t~-30 to -24 hours. Locally, without looking at Mars or
using instruments, repeated small earthquakes, rockfalls, changing springs, and
pond seiches become difficult to dismiss around t=-24 to -18 hours. By t=-12 hours,
recurrent earthquakes, metre-scale regional motion, and abnormal pressure and wind
make denial impossible. Static-ground intuition fails around t=-6 to -4 hours.


WHERE THE RELIABILITY TRANSITION OCCURS
---------------------------------------

The cold-Earth recast matters enormously until approximately +30 to +60 minutes:

- Before impact, it controls rock strength, fracture, landslides, atmosphere and
  ocean response.
- During the first shock, it controls whether the craton fragments, melts or
  vaporizes locally.
- After the Gold Butte parcel has been shocked to multi-GPa pressures and moved at
  hundreds of metres per second, its original 300 K versus the SPH model’s
  artificial 2,000 K surface temperature becomes secondary for the bulk trajectory.

But the simulation does not become locally accurate in every respect. Its roughly
260-km surface smoothing, absence of a crust, ocean and atmosphere, and fluid
equation of state mean that absolute local pressure, temperature, fragment size and
sky conditions remain uncertain. What becomes increasingly credible is the
large-scale conclusion: within about an hour after contact, the material that
constituted Gold Butte has entered a planetary hydrodynamic flow, and by the
following day no recognizable Sweet Grass Hills—or stable North American
surface—remains.
